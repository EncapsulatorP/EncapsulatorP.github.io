```text
Ω = W(1),  T_p(x) = Ω p^(Ω−x)
F_pq = T_q ∘ T_p
½(F_pq−F_qp)(Ω+u) = (Ω²/4)log p log q log(q/p)u² + O(u³)
```

**Why follow it.** The signed wave artwork has a measurable analytic effect behind it: order enters one derivative beyond the common linear response.

For u = ε sin θ, the quadratic term contains a constant and a second harmonic. Subtracting its mean leaves the second harmonic; halving ε reduces its leading amplitude by a factor of four.

## A sharp ceiling, and two bases for one remainder

**Exact identity / written proof · Towers & constants**

*Origin: Recovered tower proof; independently checked 21 November 2026*

The upper phase has an exact global maximum. Every smaller positive excess above one occurs at exactly two different bases.

```text
v log v = t exp(−t),  t = v log c
v_max = exp(W₀(1/e)) ≈ 1.3210997620
c_max = exp(1/v_max) ≈ 2.1317539817
```

**Why follow it.** A constant match becomes a solvable inverse problem. We can see what a match determines and what further arithmetic structure would need to explain.

The function t exp(−t) reaches its maximum 1/e at t = 1. Since v log v is increasing for v ≥ 1, this gives the sharp envelope and its attaining base.

For any 0 < r < v_max − 1, put v = 1 + r. The equation t exp(−t) = v log v has one solution below 1 and another above 1; c = exp(t/v) gives the two bases.

For the specimen r = 0.1, those bases are approximately 1.11320562853413457 and 24.3391050566861714. At the envelope the two solutions meet.

## The logistic two-cycle carries a quarter-turn matrix

**Exact identity / written proof · Dynamics & geometry**

*Origin: Recovered logistic and vanishing-response thread · 20 November 2026*

At the period-doubling boundary of the logistic two-cycle, its paired linear-response matrix squares to minus the identity.

```text
f_r(x) = rx(1−x),  r* = 1+√6
C_r = [[0, f′_r(x₋)], [f′_r(x₊), 0]]
C_r² = (4+2r−r²)I;  C_*² = −I;  C_*⁴ = I
```

**Why follow it.** The “quarter-turn” description has an exact linear-algebra meaning, and the vanishing derivative can be stated without losing the nonlinear map.

The nontrivial two-cycle is x± = (r+1 ± √((r−3)(r+1)))/(2r), for r > 3. Its derivative product is 4+2r−r².

At r*, that product is −1. Thus the derivative of f_r*⁴ is 1 at either cycle point, and (f_r*⁴ − id)′ vanishes there.

C_* is a linear-response operator with C_*⁴ = I. The nonlinear fourth iterate f_r*⁴ is not the identity map on an interval.

## Catalan’s constant at the centre of a beta disk

**Exact identity / written proof · Complex & spectral**

*Origin: Catalan–Euler–Ruelle conversation · 20 November 2026*

A Möbius change of coordinates places Catalan’s constant at the centre of a disk whose boundary is the critical line of the Dirichlet beta function.

```text
w = (s−2)/(s+1),  s = (2+w)/(1−w)
F(w) = β((2+w)/(1−w)),  F(0) = β(2) = G
Re s > 1/2 ⇔ |w| < 1
```

**Why follow it.** It joins an exact geometric coordinate change with a clearly posed zero-location question.

Comparing |s−2|² and |s+1|² gives the half-plane/disk equivalence. The inverse sends w = 0 to s = 2, where beta takes Catalan’s constant G.

Using beta’s functional equation and zero symmetry, zero-freeness of the open disk is equivalent to the critical-line assertion for beta’s nontrivial zeros. This is the Riemann hypothesis for L(s, χ₄), not a proof and not the ordinary zeta hypothesis.

A related weighted Gauss construction satisfies (L_t 1)(0) = β(2t) in its absolute-convergence domain. One vanishing evaluation is different from a vanishing operator determinant.

## A reciprocal orbit leaves an Abel signature

**Exact identity / written proof · Dynamics & geometry**

*Origin: Recovered orbit-signature conversation · 1 May 2026*

A discounted reciprocal orbit has an exact limiting signature for eventual cycles, and a distinct conditional limit for orbits tending to infinity.

```text
H_λ(n) = Σ_{k≥0} λ^k / a_k,  0 < λ < 1
A(n) = lim_{λ→1⁻}(1−λ)H_λ(n)
Cycle (1,4,2) → (1 + 1/4 + 1/2)/3 = 7/12
```

**Why follow it.** It recovers a nonstandard-looking construction whose core is a clean conditional theorem about bounded reciprocal sequences.

For an eventually periodic positive integer orbit, the finite initial segment vanishes under normalization and each cycle position receives equal limiting weight. The signature is the mean reciprocal value around the cycle.

A deterministic positive-integer orbit either repeats a state and becomes periodic, or never repeats and eventually leaves every finite set. In the latter case a_k tends to infinity, so its reciprocal sequence and normalized Abel mean tend to zero.

A nontrivial positive Collatz cycle cannot contain 1 or 2, so its entries are at least 3 and its reciprocal mean is at most 1/3. Consequently A(n) = 7/12 exactly when the standard Collatz orbit reaches 1.

This is an exact asymptotic characterization. It does not supply a finite-prefix decision rule or show that A(n) equals 7/12 for every starting value.

**Next experiment or question.** Formalize the orbit dichotomy and Abel limit, then identify what effective error control would be needed to use finite discounted sums.

## The ordinary tower that slows down near e

**Exact identity / written proof · Towers & constants**

*Origin: The Pirate Chest · recovered edition · 21 November 2026*

At the upper real tetration endpoint, the tower approaches its fixed point with a precise inverse-depth law.

```text
x₀=1,  x_(n+1)=exp(x_n/e)
x_n → e,  e−x_n ∼ 2e/n
```

**Why follow it.** It gives a sharp contrast to the uniform geometric convergence of the alternating tower.

For 0 < x < e, exp(x/e) lies above x and below e. Starting at 1, the iterates increase to the unique fixed point e.

Write δ_n=e−x_n. Taylor expansion gives δ_(n+1)=δ_n−δ_n²/(2e)+O(δ_n³), hence 1/δ_(n+1)−1/δ_n tends to 1/(2e). Averaging those increments yields nδ_n→2e.

This is the ordinary positive tower at base exp(1/e), with the stated starting value. The alternating tower is a different construction and has a different convergence mechanism.

## A phase inside the Sophomore’s dream

**Exact identity / written proof · Complex & spectral**

*Origin: June conversation recovered in The Pirate Chest*

A complex phase parameter enters an integral whose power series is available exactly.

```text
∫₀¹ u^(−a u) du = Σ_{n≥1} a^(n−1)/n^n
For the phase study: a = exp(2πiα(x²−y+1))
```

**Why follow it.** Phase returns can be understood through an exact series before the geometric pattern is interpreted.

Use u^(−au)=exp(−a u log u) for real 0<u≤1, with its continuous endpoint limit. Since |u log u|≤1/e, the exponential series can be integrated term by term for every fixed finite complex a.

The substitution u=exp(−t) gives ∫₀¹(−u log u)^k du = k!/(k+1)^(k+1). Dividing by k! and reindexing produces the displayed series.

At phase returns α(x²−y+1)∈ℤ, a=1 and the integral takes the familiar value approximately 1.291286. The identity defines what the phase picture is measuring.

## XO sectors: ten roots, five mirror pairs

**Exact identity / written proof · Complex & spectral**

*Origin: Recovered XO Atom sector conversation · 21 May 2026*

The finite polynomial algebra behind one XO sector construction decomposes explicitly into roots and, at degree ten, into opposite pairs.

```text
A_n = ℂ[x]/(x^n−q^n),  q ≠ 0
A_n ≅ ℂ^n
n=10: roots {±q ζ₅^j : j=0,…,4}
```

**Why follow it.** It retains a concrete algebraic object from the broader exploratory vocabulary and gives the mirror cancellation an exact meaning.

For q ≠ 0, x^n−q^n has n distinct complex roots: its derivative n x^(n−1) is nonzero at every root. Evaluation at those roots gives the product decomposition by the Chinese remainder theorem.

At n = 10, the roots can be grouped as qζ₅^j and −qζ₅^j. Each pair has sum zero. q = 0 is a different, non-semisimple case and is excluded.

The result is finite polynomial algebra. A physical interpretation or a claimed cohomological invariant requires its own defined model and argument.

## A tower can reorder a Fourier basis—when it is a permutation

**Exact identity / written proof · Models & methods**

*Origin: Tower / Hadamard recipes · 13–19 November 2025*

Tower-generated row indices preserve a finite Fourier basis exactly when they visit every remainder once.

```text
N ≥ 1 integer, t_j ∈ ℤ, j,k = 0,…,N−1
U_jk = N^(−1/2) exp(2πi t_j k/N)
U is unitary ⇔ t₀,…,t_(N−1) are distinct modulo N
```

**Why follow it.** The old recipe becomes a precise design gate: test the arithmetic selector before interpreting a spectral or learning effect.

The inner product of rows j and ℓ is a finite geometric sum. It is zero when t_j−t_ℓ is nonzero modulo N and one when those remainders agree.

With N rows, pairwise distinct remainders give a permutation of the ordinary Fourier basis. A repeated remainder creates duplicate rows and rank loss.

A tower selector can have short cycles modulo N. Orthogonality therefore comes from the verified permutation property, while any benefit of its ordering is a separate experiment.

## A square next door to every twin-prime product

**Exact identity / written proof · Prime pairs & arithmetic**

*Origin: Hibernating Lemmas · recovered prime-pair conversation*

Twin primes supply an adjacent (+1, 0) pattern in the Möbius function. Counting factors with multiplicity gives the exact two-way condition.

```text
μ(p(p + 2)) = +1,  μ((p + 1)²) = 0
For m ≥ 3:
Ω(m² − 1) = 2 ⇔ m − 1 and m + 1 are prime
```

**Why follow it.** It preserves the original adjacent-pattern observation and identifies the additional information needed for an exact characterization.

Distinct primes give a squarefree product with two prime factors; the next number is (p + 1)². This proves the displayed Möbius implication.

For m ≥ 3, both factors in (m − 1)(m + 1) are at least 2. Their Ω values add, so a total of two forces each factor to be prime.

The Möbius pattern alone also occurs at m = 34: 1155 = 3·5·7·11, followed by 34². The existing Disappointment Islands entry keeps this useful counterexample alongside the original identity.

*Origin: Goldbach p + q roadmap · November 2026*

Goldbach counts and twin-prime counts are coefficients of finite products built from the same prime polynomial.

